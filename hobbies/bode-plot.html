The Bode Plot, explained simply

Feed a system a pure tone and it gives back the same tone, only louder or quieter, and a little late. A Bode plot is two graphs that record exactly that for every frequency: how much louder (gain) and how late (phase). That's all it is.

1

Sine in, sine out

Think of a car's suspension. Drive slowly over a long, gentle hill and the car body follows the road exactly. Drive fast over small, rapid bumps and the body hardly moves; the springs swallow them. Same car, different frequency, different result. A Bode plot draws that behaviour for every frequency at once.

For a huge class of systems (filters, amplifiers, speakers, motors, feedback controllers) a sine wave going in always comes out as a sine wave of the same frequency. Only two things can change:

  • Size: the output can be bigger or smaller. That's the gain (magnitude), drawn on the top graph.
  • Timing: the output can be shifted earlier or later. That's the phase, measured in degrees of one cycle (360° = one full cycle) and drawn on the bottom graph.

Speaking in decibels and decades

Bode plots use two tricks that make them easy to read:

  • The frequency axis is logarithmic: each grid step is ×10, called a decade. 10 Hz → 100 Hz → 1 kHz are equal steps, so very low and very high frequencies fit on one page.
  • Gain is in decibels: dB = 20·log₁₀(output/input). That turns "multiply" into "add".

The Bode alphabet: seven building blocks

Almost every linear system is built from a few simple pieces. Learn each one's shape and you can sketch any Bode plot by hand. The slope is how fast the gain line climbs or falls, in dB per decade.

Rule of thumb: each pole bends the gain line down by 20 dB/decade and adds up to −90°. Each zero bends it up by 20 dB/decade and adds up to +90°. Count the poles and zeros and you know the slopes.
4

Build any system by adding blocks

When systems are chained, their gains multiply, which in dB means they add. Their phases add too. So the Bode plot of a complicated system is just the sum of its blocks' plots. Engineers sketch the straight-line approximation (the asymptotes) first, then round off the corners.

Resonance: a peak in frequency is ringing in time

A mass on a spring, a swing, an RLC circuit: anything that stores energy in two ways can resonate. Its damping ratio ζ (zeta) says how much friction there is. Low damping gives a tall, sharp peak on the Bode plot, and the same system rings when you give it a sudden kick (a step). Both pictures show the same physics.

6

Will it oscillate? Gain and phase margin

This is the most important practical use of Bode plots. In a feedback loop (cruise control, a thermostat, a motor controller, an op-amp) the output is measured and fed back to correct the input. If the signal comes back around the loop at least as big (gain ≥ 1, i.e. ≥ 0 dB) and exactly upside-down (phase −180°), the correction pushes in the wrong direction and keeps itself going. The loop oscillates.

Think of pushing a swing. Pushing in time with the swing, even gently, makes it go higher and higher. A feedback loop with gain ≥ 1 at −180° is pushing its own swing.

So we read the open-loop Bode plot (once around the loop) and ask how far we are from that danger point:

  • Phase margin (PM): where the gain crosses 0 dB, how many degrees is the phase above −180°? Aim for 45°–60° or more.
  • Gain margin (GM): where the phase hits −180°, how far below 0 dB is the gain? Aim for 6 dB or more.

Cheat sheet

What it is

Two graphs against log frequency: gain (dB) on top, phase (degrees) below.

dB in one line

×10 = +20 dB, ×2 ≈ +6 dB, ×0.707 = −3 dB, ×0.1 = −20 dB.

Corner frequency

Where a pole or zero starts to act: −3 dB and ±45° for a single one.

Slopes

Each pole −20 dB/decade and −90°. Each zero +20 dB/decade and +90°.

Adding

Chained systems: add their dB and add their phases.

Resonance

Small ζ gives a tall peak and ringing. ζ ≈ 0.7 is the flattest response.

Bandwidth

Frequency where the gain has dropped 3 dB below its flat value.

Stability

Open loop: phase margin ≥ 45°, gain margin ≥ 6 dB. Delay eats phase margin.
