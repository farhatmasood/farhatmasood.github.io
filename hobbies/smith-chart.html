The Smith Chart, explained simply

A Smith chart is a map. Every load you could connect to the end of a cable has exactly one spot on it, and the distance from the centre tells you how much of your signal bounces back. That is the whole idea. The rest is learning to read the map.

1

Signals bounce when things don't match

Picture a rope. Tie one end to a wall and flick it: the wave runs to the wall and comes back upside down. Tie it to a ring that slides freely on a pole: the wave comes back right way up. Tie it to an identical rope that goes on forever: the wave just keeps going and nothing comes back.

Electrical signals on a cable behave in the same way. The cable has a characteristic impedance Z0, usually 50 Ω; that is "the rope". Whatever sits at the end (an antenna, an amplifier, a resistor) is the load ZL; that is "what the rope is tied to". If ZL equals Z0, the signal flows into the load. If not, part of it bounces back toward the source.

Why it matters: reflected power never reaches the antenna, wastes transmitter power, and can even damage amplifiers. RF engineers spend much of their time making ZL look like Z0. The Smith chart is the tool they use to do it.
2

One number describes the bounce: Γ

The reflection coefficient Γ (gamma) says how big the bounce is compared with the wave that went in:

Γ = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0)Same load as the cable → top is zero → no bounce.
Load on a 50 Ω cableΓWhat happens
Short circuit (0 Ω)−1Everything bounces back, flipped (the wall)
Open circuit (∞)+1Everything bounces back, not flipped (the free ring)
50 Ω0Nothing bounces: perfect match
100 Ω+0.33A third of the voltage bounces; Γ² = 11% of the power
25 Ω−0.33Same size bounce, but flipped

Loads are usually not plain resistors. Coils and capacitors delay the bounce, so in general Γ is a complex number with two parts:

  • Size |Γ|, from 0 to 1: how much bounces back.
  • Angle ∠Γ: how much the bounce is delayed or flipped.
Here is the trick. For any real-world (passive) load, |Γ| is never bigger than 1. So every possible load lands somewhere inside a circle of radius 1. Draw that circle and you have a Smith chart.

To make one chart work for every cable, engineers divide by Z0 first: z = ZL/Z0 (lower-case z means "normalised"). Then Γ = (z − 1)/(z + 1), and the centre of the chart is always z = 1.

3

Squeezing infinity into a circle

An impedance has two parts: z = r + jx. The resistance r absorbs power and is never negative. The reactance x stores energy: positive for coils (inductive), negative for capacitors (capacitive). Plot all possible values and you get an infinite half-plane, which is no use on paper.

The Γ formula bends that infinite half-plane into the unit circle. Straight grid lines become circles:

Reading the map

🎯 Centre

z = 1 (50 Ω on a 50 Ω cable). Perfect match, no bounce.

⬅️ Left edge

Short circuit. Γ = −1.

➡️ Right edge

Open circuit. Γ = +1.

➖ Horizontal line

Pure resistors: no coil, no capacitor.

⬆️ Top half

Inductive loads (behave like a coil).

⬇️ Bottom half

Capacitive loads (behave like a capacitor).

⭕ Outer rim

No resistance at all: pure L or C. Everything bounces.

📏 Distance from centre

Equals |Γ|. Closer to the centre means a better match.

5

Walking down a cable = turning around the centre

This is where the Smith chart earns its keep. Put some cable between you and the load. The amount of bounce stays the same (a lossless cable doesn't absorb anything), but the bounce arrives back later, so its angle changes. On the chart that is a simple rotation:

Rule: moving away from the load toward the generator turns the dot clockwise around the centre. Half a wavelength of cable is one full turn.

Two famous results fall straight out of the picture:

  • λ/2 cable = one full turn: the input looks exactly like the load.
  • λ/4 cable = half a turn: the dot jumps to the opposite side. A short looks like an open, and 100 Ω looks like 25 Ω (Zin = Z0²/ZL). This is the quarter-wave transformer.
6

Adding a coil or capacitor = sliding along a circle

To fix a mismatch we add coils (L) and capacitors (C). Each one moves the dot along one of the chart's circles:

  • Series part (in line with the load): it only changes the reactance x, so the dot slides along its own resistance circle. A coil moves it up (clockwise), a capacitor moves it down.
  • Shunt part (across the load, to ground): parallel parts are easier to add as admittance y = 1/z = g + jb. A shunt part only changes b, so the dot slides along a constant-conductance circle. A capacitor moves it down (clockwise), a coil moves it up.
The admittance trick: y = 1/z is the same point turned 180° around the centre. So the admittance grid is just the Smith chart flipped left-to-right. Overlay both grids (often drawn in two colours) and you can switch between series and shunt thinking without any arithmetic.

7

The matching game: get the dot to the centre

Matching means adding parts until the load looks like Z0, so the dot sits at the centre. With two parts (an "L-network") the recipe is always the same:

  1. Use the first part to slide onto the green circle. This is the one circle that passes through the centre.
  2. Use the second part to slide along the green circle into the centre.

8

Cheat sheet

What it is

A map of every load, placed by its reflection Γ. Inside the unit circle means passive load.

Goal

Move the dot to the centre (z = 1). That is a perfect match with no bounce.

Distance = bounce

Radius = |Γ|. Circles around the centre have constant VSWR and return loss.

Cable = rotation

Clockwise toward the generator. λ/2 = one full turn; λ/4 = flip to the opposite side.

Series L / C

Slide along a constant-r circle. L clockwise (up), C counter-clockwise (down).

Shunt C / L

Slide along a constant-g circle. C clockwise (down), L counter-clockwise (up).

z ↔ y

Turn the point 180° around the centre to switch between impedance and admittance.

Where it's used

Antenna tuning, amplifier design, PCB trace and connector design, network-analyser screens, MRI RF coil matching.
